The North House is ninety years older than the co-op, and it was built two blocks from where it stands. George Hebard Maxwell, a young lawyer, had it put up in 1885 at 2401 Piedmont Way, on the corner of Channing, to plans by the architect Clinton Day.[^1][^2] In 1903 it was sold and moved east along Channing to 2405 Prospect Street.[^3]

## On Piedmont Way
![The Maxwell house in 1895](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Maxwell_House_1895.png "The house at 2401 Piedmont Way in 1895, eight years before the move.")

Piedmont Way was Frederick Law Olmsted's parkway for the College of California's homesites, a tree-lined avenue with a small round park where it crosses Channing.[^4] Maxwell's house stood on the southeast corner of that crossing. Maxwell was listed there, "res cor Piedmont Way and Channing," in the 1884 city directory.[^5] The 1903 Sanborn fire-insurance map shows the house still there: a two-and-a-half-story frame house on the corner lot, with the Prospect Street lot where it would end up still empty.

![Sanborn fire-insurance map of Channing Way between Piedmont and Prospect, 1903](local:sanborn-1903.png "Sanborn map, 1903. The Maxwell house is the large dwelling on the southeast corner of Channing and Piedmont, by the number 2403. Its future lot is the empty corner across Prospect, below 5381.")

Clinton Day (1847–1916) was a child of the college himself. He graduated from the College of California in 1868, the year before it became the University of California, and his father, Sherman Day, was one of its founders.[^2][^6] He went on to design the City of Paris building and the Union Trust building in San Francisco, Memorial Church at Stanford and several early buildings on the Berkeley campus.[^2]

## The man it was built for
![George Hebard Maxwell](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/George_Hebard_Maxwell.jpg "George Hebard Maxwell (1860–1946).")

Maxwell (1860–1946) finished at the University of California, worked as a court stenographer and then practiced law.[^7] In the 1890s he became the Bay Area's loudest advocate for irrigation and cooperative farm colonies. He founded the National Reclamation Association in 1899 and, with Representative Francis Newlands of Nevada, wrote the National Reclamation Act, which Theodore Roosevelt signed on June 17, 1902.[^7][^8] The act paid for irrigation across seventeen western states and created what became the Bureau of Reclamation.[^8]

He soon turned on how it was run, saying a public service had become a business for land speculators and agribusiness, and spent his later years promoting "homecrofts": small family plots that fed the people who worked them.[^7] A house of people who share their food, labor and land now lives in his living room.

## Up the hill
The Maxwells sold the house in 1903 to Fred and Alice Clark, a real estate developer and his wife, who moved it two blocks east to its present lot on Prospect.[^3] The route was a straight line: east along the south side of Channing, past Warring, to the southeast corner of Channing and Prospect. Moving a wooden house was ordinary work in Berkeley then, as the blocks near campus filled in. The lumberman O. S. Johnson bought it a few years later.[^3]

In 1906 the lot next door got a house of its own, built for the attorney Phillip Gleason Galpin, who lived there until his death in 1911. That is the South House, 2415 Prospect.[^3]

## In the house
- Council meets every week in the North House living room: Maxwell's.[^9]
- The North and South Houses were built for different families, a generation apart, and were joined into one place only when the co-op bought them in 1975.
- The house has been student housing for most of the century since the move, first as fraternity and sorority houses from the 1920s, then as the co-op.[^3]
