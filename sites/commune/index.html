Where power sits

The Congress of every comrade sits at the top. Each organ below answers to the ones above it, and every officer can be recalled by the Congress at any moment.

    How a law is made

    1. A delegate lays a bill in the Council, stating the law's type, its tags, who enforces it and on whom.
    2. The Council votes for seven days, in public. A law needs a simple majority of delegates; an organic law, two thirds.
    3. The Congress may intervene. For fourteen days after it passes, one comrade in ten can petition to send the bill to every comrade, who then decide it.
    4. It comes into force, unless petitioned, and goes into the statute book with who made it and how.
    5. Or the Congress acts alone. Any comrade may put a law, a repeal or a recall straight to the Congress. Only the Congress amends the Fundamental Law.
    6. Decrees. The Presidium may issue decrees, all three members signing, at most ten a season. The Council or the Congress can repeal any of them.

    Labour and the Plan

    One verified hour of work pays one labour voucher, whatever the work and whoever does it. Vouchers cannot be sold or given away, only spent.

            The symbols of the Commune

            Article 12 of the Fundamental Law.

            The flag

            A round table seen from above, so that no seat is at its head. Each cream chair is a comrade; every chair is the same size. The gold star at the centre is what the comrades hold in common.

            The chair at the top is empty and outlined in gold. It is kept for whoever comes next.

            Proportions 3:2. Red #A3241C, cream #F4EAD8, gold #D9A62E.

            The motto

            From each according to ability, to each according to need.

            De chacun selon ses capacités, à chacun selon ses besoins. Karl Marx, 1875.

            The anthem

            The Long Table. Sung slowly, standing, in any key the room can manage.

            Come down when the kettle is singing,
            there’s a chair with your name on the wall;
            what we carry, we carry together,
            and the table is long enough for all.

            Pull up a chair, comrade, pull up a chair,
            the bread is still warm and the reckoning’s fair;
            from each what they can, and to each what they lack,
            and one chair kept empty, with no name on its back.

            We counted the hours by lamplight,
            every hour the same as the rest:
            the drafting of laws and the washing of pots,
            and the grease trap, which nobody likes best.

            And if ever we part on a question,
            we will part as we sat, side by side,
            with a copy of all that we did in our pockets,
            and the door of the kitchen left wide.

            The calendar and holidays

            …

              The Commune counts its years from its founding and divides each into four seasons of ninety days, which are also the terms of its delegates and commissars. The odd days left over at the end of a year belong to nobody and are spent on the washing up.

              Honours

                Conferred by the Presidium, all three signing, at most twelve a season.

                Forms and customs

                • Address. “Comrade”, or a person's name, as they prefer.
                • Currency. The labour voucher, counted in tenths of an hour. Written 3.5 v.
                • The paper of record. The Gazette, kept by the Commissar of Enlightenment, in the Gazette channel.
                • The seal. A round seal with the gold star, on the letterheads of the Presidium and of the Commissariat of External Affairs.
                • Criticism and self-criticism. A channel where anything may be said about anyone, oneself included, and nobody is punished for it.
                • Leaving. Any comrade may leave; any minority may leave together, taking the whole record with them (Article 2.2).
