# Prologue

The Digital Republic Society is a small, private association of people who intend to start cooperatives, and who want to do it with their own savings, their own rules and their own records. Most of us are students or recent graduates. Many of us live in cooperative houses already, and know from experience that a group of people can own and run a building well. What we lack is the money to buy one, the knowledge of how it is done, and other people in the same position to do it with.

The Society exists to supply those three things over time. It is a place to plan, a way to save together, and a record of everything we decide. This manifesto sets out what the Society is for, how it works, what it asks of its members and what it will never do. It is meant to be read once in full by every new member and argued with by all of them afterwards.

# I. Why the Society Exists

A cooperative is an organization owned and governed by the people who use it. In a housing cooperative, the residents own the corporation that owns the building, and each household has one vote in how it is run. In a worker cooperative, the people who work in the business own it and decide how it is managed. Cooperatives of both kinds have existed for nearly two centuries and house or employ millions of people around the world. In the United States they remain rare, mostly because starting one requires a large sum of money at a moment when the people who want to start it have very little.

The usual pattern is familiar to anyone who has tried. A group of tenants learns that their building is for sale. They meet, they agree that they would like to buy it, and they discover that a bank will lend only part of the price, to an organization that does not yet exist, after an appraisal that takes weeks. By the time they have incorporated and found a lender, the building has been sold to a buyer who could close in thirty days. The same thing happens to people who want to start a bakery, a repair shop or a clinic as a cooperative. The idea is sound, and the timing defeats it.

Two things change the timing. The first is money saved in advance by people who already trust one another, so that a deposit or a first payment is available when an opportunity appears. The second is preparation done in advance: the legal documents drafted, the rules agreed, the members known to each other, the arithmetic done. Neither can be produced in a few weeks. Both can be built slowly by a group that meets regularly and keeps good records. That is the work of the Society.

# II. What a Cooperative Needs Before It Exists

Every cooperative we have studied needed five things before it opened, and most of the ones that failed lacked at least one of them.

1. **People.** A cooperative needs enough members who are committed to it, who can work together, and who will still be there in three years. The hardest part of starting a cooperative is usually finding these people and learning whether they can disagree without falling apart.
2. **Money.** A housing cooperative needs a down payment, closing costs and a reserve for repairs. A worker cooperative needs equipment, rent and enough cash to pay its members until it earns. In either case, some of the money must come from the members themselves, because lenders want to see that the members have something at stake.
3. **A legal form.** A cooperative has to be a corporation, a limited liability company or some other entity the law recognizes, with bylaws that say who the members are and how they decide. The choice of form affects taxes, liability and how the members can leave.
4. **Knowledge.** Someone has to understand appraisals, mortgages, insurance, payroll, permits and the many small rules that apply to a building or a business. Few people know all of this at the start. A group can learn it together, and it is much cheaper to learn before a deal than during one.
5. **A record.** The members need a reliable account of who paid what, who owns what, what was decided and when. Cooperatives that keep poor records tend to end in arguments about money, and arguments about money are the most common reason cooperatives fail.

The Society cannot provide any of these on a member's behalf. It provides a setting in which members can build all five together, slowly, before they need them.

# III. The Investment Club as a Seed

The Society's main tool for saving together is the investment club, organized as a California general partnership. Because this choice matters, and because it carries real obligations, every member should understand it.

## What a general partnership is

A general partnership is the simplest form of business the law recognizes. Under California law, two or more people who carry on a business together for profit as co-owners form a partnership, whether or not they file anything with the state. Each partner shares in the profits and losses, each has a say in how the partnership is run, and, in a general partnership, each partner is personally liable for the partnership's debts. A partnership agreement, written and signed by the partners, sets out how much each partner contributes, how decisions are made, how profits are shared, and what happens when a partner leaves.

## Why a club, and why this form

An investment club is a partnership whose business is investing its partners' contributions. The partners pay in regularly, usually a small fixed amount each month, and vote on what to buy and sell. The club's assets are held in a brokerage account in the partnership's name. Each partner's share of the club is measured in units, bought at the club's current value per unit whenever the partner contributes, so that a partner who joins late or pays in more is treated fairly.

We use this form for three reasons. It lets a group of people save together under rules they have written and signed, with every contribution and every trade on the record. It teaches the partners to make financial decisions jointly, which is exactly the skill a cooperative needs. And because every partner takes part in managing the club, the partners are not passive investors relying on someone else's efforts. That distinction matters under securities law, and it is one of the reasons the club must stay genuinely managed by all of its partners.

## What it is not

The club is not a bank, and it does not take deposits from anyone outside it. It is not a fund that promises returns. It does not pay any partner to manage other partners' money. Its partners can lose money, as any investor can, and in a general partnership they are personally responsible for its debts. For this reason the club does not borrow, does not trade on margin and does not sell anything short.

## How a club becomes a cooperative

The club is a seed. When its partners have saved enough, found the people they need and done the preparation described in this manifesto, they can use what they have built to form the cooperative itself. That usually means forming a new entity, a cooperative corporation or a limited liability company, and moving the partners' capital into it according to a plan the partners adopt by joint resolution. Some clubs will become cooperatives. Others will lend to cooperatives, or simply give their partners a disciplined way to save. All of these outcomes are acceptable.

> *None of the above is legal or financial advice, and the Society does not give any. The partnership agreement the Society provides is a draft. Before anyone signs it or pays anything in, the partners should have it reviewed by a California attorney, and should talk to an accountant about the club's tax filings, which a partnership must make every year.*

# IV. The Board

The Society's discussion board is where planning happens. It is divided into a small number of rooms, each for one part of the work.

- **New co-ops.** Proposals for cooperatives members want to start, at any stage, from a sentence to a business plan. A member who posts a proposal is asking for criticism, and should receive it.
- **Buildings and places.** Buildings for sale, commercial spaces, land trusts, and what members have learned about particular neighborhoods and landlords.
- **Money and clubs.** The investment club, other ways of financing a cooperative, lenders, grants, solidarity funds, and the arithmetic of particular deals.
- **Law and paperwork.** Legal forms, bylaws, agreements, permits, taxes and insurance, and what members have learned from lawyers and accountants.
- **The workshop.** The Society's own tools: its software, its record, its letters and its website.

The rules of the board are short. Members write under their own names. They criticize proposals, not people. They mark clearly anything that is a guess rather than a fact. They do not post anything a person told them in confidence, or anything about another member's finances, without that member's agreement. The Warden, elected by the Conclave, may hide a post that breaks these rules, and every hidden post, and the reason for hiding it, stays on the record.

# V. Secrecy, and Its Limits

The Society is a private association. Its record, its board and its wiki are open only to members, and its membership list is not published. This privacy has practical reasons. Members discuss their own savings and debts. They discuss buildings before the owners know anyone is interested, and negotiations go better when the other side does not know the buyers' plans. And people speak more freely, and admit more ignorance, when they know who is listening.

The Society's privacy has firm limits.

1. Any member may say publicly that they are a member, and may describe the Society's purposes in general terms. This manifesto is not a secret from members' families, partners or lawyers.
2. No member may be asked to keep secret anything that would harm another person, that the law requires them to report, or that concerns their own safety.
3. The Society takes part in no initiation or ceremony that harms, humiliates or pressures anyone. A member who sees one reports it, and the Conclave deals with it.
4. Every decision of the Society is recorded and visible to every member. There is no inner circle whose decisions the members cannot read. Officers act only within powers written down and granted by the Conclave.

The Society keeps its record private because its members' affairs are their own, and keeps nothing from its own members.

# VI. The Record

Everything the Society decides is kept in a signed record, using software its members can read, run and change. Each member holds a full copy of the record on their own device, and each act in it is signed with the key of the member who made it. A copy that has been altered is detected when it is compared with the others. The record is replicated privately among members' machines and is never published.

The record holds the membership, every motion and vote, every post on the board, every version of every wiki page, the investment club's contributions and trades, and the Society's letters. It is the Society's memory. When members disagree about what was decided, the record settles it. When an officer acts, the record shows what they did and under which grant. When a member leaves, they may take a copy of everything that was theirs to see.

Letters are a small part of the record that deserves mention, because members will receive them. A letter of the Society is issued on its letterhead only by the body entitled to issue it: the Conclave by vote, or an officer or committee through a power the Conclave granted. A letter shows the signatures of the members who agreed to it. Every page of the wiki, this one included, can also be read as a letter, set on the Society's letterhead, so that members can print it, file it or hand it to a lawyer.

# VII. The Obligations of Members

A member of the Society agrees to the following, by signing the Obligation when they join.

1. **To take part.** Members read the board, vote in the Conclave, and contribute what they know. The Society depends on members who do the work of preparing, which is slow and unglamorous.
2. **To keep confidences.** Members do not repeat what other members share in confidence, and do not disclose the Society's plans for particular buildings or businesses before the members concerned agree.
3. **To keep their keys.** A member's signing key is their signature. Members keep it safe, and tell the Society at once if they think it has been lost or copied, so that it can be replaced.
4. **To be honest about money.** Members who join a club pay what they promised, on time, or say plainly that they cannot. They do not exaggerate what they know about an investment, and they do not hide a conflict of interest.
5. **To leave cleanly.** A member may leave at any time. Leaving ends every obligation except the confidences already given and any amount still owed to a club under its partnership agreement.

# VIII. How a Plan Becomes a Cooperative

The Society expects most plans to move through five stages. Not every plan passes through all of them, and many will stop along the way, which is no failure. A plan that stops at the second stage has still taught its members something.

1. **An idea.** A member posts a proposal on the board: what cooperative, where, for whom, and roughly what it would cost.
2. **A circle.** Members who are interested form a working group. They meet, study the proposal, and decide whether to go further. They write down what they agree and what they do not.
3. **A club.** The circle forms an investment club, or joins an existing one, and begins to save. The partners sign the partnership agreement, open an account in the partnership's name, and keep every contribution on the record.
4. **An offer.** When the club and its partners are ready, and a suitable building or business is available, the partners prepare an offer: the price, the financing, the legal form of the cooperative, and the plan for moving the club's capital into it. The plan is adopted by joint resolution of the partners.
5. **A cooperative.** The cooperative is formed, the purchase closes, and the members begin to run it. The cooperative is its own entity, with its own members and its own record. The Society remains a place where its founders can ask for help, and where they can help the next group.

# IX. What the Society Will Not Do

Some rules are best stated as refusals. The Society will not do any of the following, and no officer or committee has the power to do them.

1. It will not take money from anyone who is not a member of the club concerned, or invite the public to invest.
2. It will not promise any member a return on anything.
3. It will not pressure anyone to join a club, to contribute more than they can afford, or to stay in a club they want to leave.
4. It will not buy buildings to resell them at a profit, or help any member do so. The purpose of the Society is to create cooperatives that last, and speculation works against that purpose.
5. It will not keep any decision from its own members.
6. It will not hold any member's signing key on their behalf, or sign anything in a member's name.

# Closing

The Society's founders do not expect it to grow quickly, and do not want it to. A small group that meets often, saves steadily and keeps careful records can do more than a large one that does none of these things. If the Society works, its success will be measured in cooperatives that exist because its members prepared for them, and in members who learned enough here to help the next group.
